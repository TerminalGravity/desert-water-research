Scenario · Lake Havasu City and the Mohave Mountains, Arizona

Can a 4-inch desert catch, keep, and bank its own water?

Lake Havasu City sits on the Colorado River below the Mohave Mountains, whose highest point is a 5,100-ft granite peak. A normal year brings a little over four inches of rain^[1]. A pan of open water in the region can lose about 100 inches a year to evaporation^[3]. Inland, the Hualapai Valley aquifer is estimated to lose about 50,000 acre-feet a year more than it receives^[14].

This is a desk study built from public data and published sources. It follows one path through the problem: map the catchment, estimate what runs off, keep it out of the sun, and push it underground. Nothing here has been measured in the field yet. Read what this is and isn't before using any number.

Method · Read this first

What this is and isn't

This is

  • A desk study compiled from public data and published sources, each cited.
  • A 3D view of public USGS 3DEP elevation data, rendered as a surface with 1.5× vertical exaggeration^[64]. The data was exported from the 3DEP Elevation ImageServer and resampled to about 50 m (3D mesh) and 25 m (drainage analysis) grids.
  • A computed drainage network (D8 flow accumulation) on that resampled DEM.
  • Runoff estimates from a formula: area × the NOAA 1991–2020 rainfall normal (4.16 in/yr at Lake Havasu City)^[1] × 27,154 gal/in/acre × an assumed runoff coefficient.
  • A proposed research path. Its steps have not been carried out yet.

This isn't

  • No drone-captured terrain model or photogrammetry exists yet. Drone capture is a proposed next step (roadmap).
  • Runoff estimates are not field measurements. They are formula outputs, shown as ranges.
  • No rain gauge or measured runoff exists for the Mohave Mountains in this study. The rainfall figure is the valley-floor normal at the city.
  • The catchment boundaries are computed from a resampled DEM. They have not been field-checked.
  • Not engineering, legal or water-rights advice.
Reflection · Open questions

What we don't know yet

  • Mountain rainfall: What does it actually rain on Crossman Peak? No long-term high-elevation rain gauge was found in the Mohave Mountains.
  • Runoff rates: No measured runoff coefficients exist here for local granite and volcanic slopes. The 0.20–0.60 range used in the estimate tool is assumed.
  • Terrain detail: There is no drone photogrammetry yet. Free 1 m lidar exists (see existing data), but it cannot show current rock surfaces, pooling or flow marks after a storm.
  • Water rights: What is the legal status of capturing flow from washes, as opposed to roofs and bare slopes?
  • Policy: Would Hualapai or Sacramento Valley meet AMA criteria if subsidence emerges?

How to answer these: see the roadmap and the open runoff validation protocol.

What each source can and can't tell us

  • 3DEP 1 m lidar: Can map drainage networks, slope and catchment boundaries across the whole range. It cannot show current surface conditions after a storm, because it is a single 2023–24 snapshot^[58].
  • Sentinel-2 / Landsat: Can show vegetation, land cover and decades of change. At 10–30 m they cannot resolve individual washes, check dams or pooling.
  • Sentinel-1 SAR: Can see through monsoon cloud and detect surface change. It does not produce a usable visual image of small structures.
  • Commercial 30–50 cm: Can show larger features and be tasked. It is not centimeter-scale, and it costs money per km².
  • Drones: Can deliver 1–3 cm orthomosaics on demand, showing rock type, vegetation and standing water^[77][79]. Drone lidar can penetrate vegetation to bare earth; photogrammetry models only the visible surface^[78]. Drones cover only small areas per day^[77].
Recommendation: Use free 3DEP lidar for range-scale catchment analysis. Use drone photogrammetry to design infrastructure in individual sub-catchments. Use drone lidar where vegetation or complex terrain hides the ground: basin first, site second.
Path · Next steps

Roadmap: turning open questions into a plan

  1. Drone photogrammetry flight plan for sub-catchment D1 (about 118 acres, below the radio towers) and later the Falls Springs Wash catchment. Both footprints are shown in the 3D panel.
    • Overlap: Plan at least 80% forward and 70% side overlap. Raise it on steep slopes, because effective overlap drops as the terrain rises toward the camera. Add oblique images^[44].
    • Altitude: Fly terrain-following from the 1 m 3DEP DEM so height above ground, and therefore ground sample distance (GSD), stays constant^[44]. GSD = pixel size × distance ÷ focal length; typical 1–3 cm at drone altitudes^[77].
    • Ground control: Use well-distributed, non-collinear GCPs including the perimeter, at least 4–5^[45]. On inaccessible slopes, use RTK/PPK imagery plus at least 3 GCPs at the base or top^[46].
    • Rules: FAA Part 107, with a 400 ft above-ground ceiling^[47]. Lake Havasu City Airport (KHII) is non-towered, in Class G airspace^[49][50]. Check current airspace and NOTAMs before each flight, and use LAANC if any part of the area is controlled^[48].
  2. Rain gauge network: Place tipping-bucket gauges along the mountain-front elevation transect. Sites: the valley NOAA station (existing)^[1]; Falls Springs Wash at about 1,750 ft^[37]; the road end at about 3,275 ft^[38]; near the towers at about 4,700 ft^[35][38]; plus two inside sub-catchment D1. Details are in the protocol.
  3. Runoff coefficient validation: Build a small test catchment with a 90° V-notch or rectangular weir and a stage logger, or use bucket-and-timer for small flows^[56][81]. Compute C = runoff volume ÷ (rainfall depth × catchment area). Full method: validation protocol.
  4. Permitting checklist (confirm each item with the agency):
    • BLM Lake Havasu Field Office: land-use authorization for any structure or instrument on public land^[51]. BLM has acted as a cooperating agency on NEPA review at Crossman Peak before^[52].
    • NEPA review for any federal authorization^[52].
    • ADWR: Underground Storage Facility and Water Storage permits for recharge^[53][54]. Surface-water rights status for capturing wash flow is an open question.
    • ADEQ: water-quality and aquifer-protection determinations tied to recharge^[54][7].
    • Mohave County Development Services: floodplain and grading rules. to confirm
    • SHPO / tribal consultation: a cultural-resources survey under NHPA Section 106 for any federal undertaking^[55].
Context

Why this matters: an over-allocated river

The 1922 Colorado River Compact apportions 7.5 million acre-feet (maf) a year to the Upper Basin and 7.5 maf to the Lower Basin. Arizona's share of the Lower Basin is 2.8 maf^[40]. For calendar year 2026, Reclamation declared a Tier 1 Lower Basin shortage. It projected Lake Mead at 1,055.88 ft on January 1, which cut Arizona's apportionment by 320,000 acre-ft and required a further 192,000 acre-ft drought-contingency contribution: 512,000 acre-ft in total^[39]. Proposals for the post-2026 rules have been reported to include combined Lower Basin cuts of up to 3 maf a year41 reported.

Lake Havasu alone loses an estimated 139,626 acre-ft a year to evaporation^[4]. That is why this study is framed as an engineering opportunity: every gallon captured and stored before it evaporates is a gallon that doesn't need to come from an over-allocated river.

Path · Step 1: Terrain

Terrain data and catchment analysis

The Mohave Mountains rise east of Lake Havasu City to Crossman Peak (34.5489° N, 114.1885° W; 5,102 ft / 1,555 m)^[5]. The radio towers sit on the west ridge at about 4,712 ft^[35][36]. Steep, thin-soiled slopes shed water quickly into washes such as Falls Springs Wash, which crosses the alluvial fans toward the city^[37].

What has been done (desk analysis): USGS 3DEP elevation was exported from the 3DEP ImageServer, which serves the best available 3DEP DEM; 1 m lidar DEMs exist for this area^[63][64]. The export was resampled to about 25 m. Depressions were filled with a priority-flood algorithm. Then D8 flow direction and flow accumulation were computed, and the catchment above the Falls Springs Wash GNIS point was delineated: about 12.98 km² (3,206 acres), spanning about 529–1,546 m. The resampling smooths the summit: 1,546 m on this grid versus the published 1,555 m^[5].

Proposed (not done): Drone photogrammetry of sub-catchment D1, plus a re-run of the analysis on the full 1 m lidar.

Path · Step 2: Harvest

Rainwater harvesting from monsoon runoff on steep slopes

Lake Havasu's rain comes in two seasons: winter frontal storms (December–March, the wettest months) and the July–September monsoon. Monthly normals include January 0.75 in, August 0.38 in and June 0.01 in^[1].

Design notes for steep slopes

  • Capture at slope breaks or narrow bedrock channels, where a small weir or diversion can intercept flow with little fill.
  • Settle out sediment before it reaches cisterns. Monsoon runoff carries heavy bedload.
  • Size storage for individual storms, not annual totals: a single 1-inch storm equals about 24% of the 4.16 in annual normal (arithmetic from^[1]).
  • Diverting flow in a defined watercourse may raise surface-water-right questions. open question

Path · Step 4: Bank it

Managed aquifer recharge: check dams, gabions, infiltration basins

  • Check dams: In Arizona's Chiricahua Mountains, check dams reduced peak runoff but not overall flow volume^[27].
  • Gabions: In one ephemeral stream, gabions raised infiltrated volume by about 10% on average for a roughly 5-hour flow. Modeled outcomes ranged from no effect to about 255%^[26].
  • Infiltration basins: In the Hualapai basin, an infiltration project was credited with 1,200 acre-ft/yr in the 2021 USGS budget, but it was excluded from the 2024 county update^[14].
Reflection: in these valleys, natural aquifer recharge is under 1% of the precipitation that falls on them: 13,100 of about 1.74 million acre-ft/yr^[34].
Evidence · Local precedent

Lake Havasu City's reuse-water and recharge program

Robert McCulloch founded Lake Havasu City in 1963. Its first golf course opened in 1967^[10]. Close to half of the city's effluent, about 2,000 acre-ft/yr, has gone to irrigating golf courses and other turf^[9]. About 1,850 acre-ft was sold for irrigation in 2018^[11].

In December 2008 the city began injecting ultrafiltered effluent into four 180-ft vadose-zone wells. It describes this as the first treated-wastewater recharge project on the lower Colorado River^[7]. The pilot used 180 acre-ft^[7]. The 2022 master plan recommends about 2 MGD of additional vadose wells^[8].

Correction: “since 1967”. corrected No source supports a recharge program dating from 1967; that is the year the city's first golf course opened^[10]. Recharge dates to December 2008^[7].

Evidence · Estimate tool

Estimating runoff from mountain slopes

These are estimates, not measurements. They use the NOAA valley-floor rainfall normal^[1] and an assumed runoff coefficient. No rain gauge or runoff measurement exists for the Mohave Mountains.

Derivation: 1 acre = 43,560 ft². 43,560 × (1/12 ft) × 7.48052 gal/ft³ = 27,154 gal per inch per acre. At 4.16 in and C = 0.40, one acre gives 45,184 gal/yr. With the older 1981–2010 normal of 3.84 in^[2], it gives 41,709 gal/yr.

Check: “1 acre ≈ 40,000 gal/yr at 40%”. arithmetic holds The claim is arithmetically consistent, but it is still an estimate. Across the assumed 0.20–0.60 coefficient range, one acre at 4.16 in gives about 22,600–67,800 gal/yr.

Evaporation losses in extreme heat

July normal highs at Lake Havasu City reach 109 °F, with about 178 days a year at or above 90 °F^[1]. Pan evaporation at the nearest long record is about 101.5 in/yr, peaking at 13.3 in in June^[3].

Policy context

The 1980 Groundwater Management Act created ADWR. It designated four AMAs and two INAs^[30]. Today there are six AMAs, including Santa Cruz and Douglas, and three INAs, including the Hualapai Valley^[30][31].
