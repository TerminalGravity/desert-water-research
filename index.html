Scenario · Lake Havasu City & the Mohave Mountains, Arizona

Can a 4‑inch desert catch, keep, and bank its own water?

The scenario: a city of the Colorado River sits beneath a 5,100‑ft granite range. A little over four inches of rain falls in a normal year[1], while a pan of open water in the region can lose roughly 100 inches[3]. Inland, the Hualapai Valley aquifer is drawn down by an estimated ~50,000 acre‑feet a year more than it receives[14]. This notebook follows one path through the problem: map the catchment, harvest what runs off, keep it out of the sun, and push it underground, then weigh what the evidence supports.

Path · Step 1: Terrain

3D terrain, photogrammetry and catchment analysis

The Mohave Mountains rise east of Lake Havasu City to Crossman Peak (34.5489° N, 114.1885° W; about 5,102 ft / 1,555 m per Peakbagger [5]; the user‑cited ~5,103 ft is within survey rounding). Steep, thin‑soiled granite and volcanic slopes shed water quickly into washes that cross the alluvial fans toward the lake. To harvest or recharge that water you first need to know where it goes.

1 · Base DEM

USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) provides public lidar‑derived and 1/3 arc‑second (~10 m) DEMs, downloadable through The National Map [6].

2 · Drone photogrammetry

For candidate sub‑catchments: plan overlapping grid flights (commonly 70–80% overlap), set surveyed ground control points, process with structure‑from‑motion (e.g. OpenDroneMap) to a cm‑scale DSM and orthomosaic. method plan

3 · Hydro‑conditioning

Fill sinks / breach depressions, compute D8 or D‑infinity flow direction, then flow accumulation (e.g. WhiteboxTools, TauDEM, GRASS r.watershed).

4 · Delineation

Snap pour points at proposed check‑dam or cistern sites; derive contributing area, slope distribution and stream order for each catchment.

Path · Step 2: Harvest

Rainwater harvesting from monsoon runoff on steep slopes

Lake Havasu's rain arrives in two seasons: winter frontal storms (Dec–Mar, the wettest months) and the July–September monsoon. Normals: Jan 0.75 in, Aug 0.38 in, Jun 0.01 in [1]. Monsoon cells are brief and intense; on bare rock a high fraction runs off, but much of it also arrives as sediment‑laden flash flow.

Precipitation: NOAA 1991–2020 normals, station USC00024761 [1]. Evaporation: WRCC monthly pan averages for Davis Dam, ~60 mi north, 1948–61 [3] (no long pan record exists at Lake Havasu City itself; treat as a regional proxy).

Design notes for steep slopes

  • Capture at slope breaks or narrow bedrock channels where a small weir/diversion can intercept flow with little fill.
  • Pre‑settle sediment (trash racks, settling pools) before cisterns; monsoon runoff carries heavy bedload.
  • Size for event volume, not annual totals: a rare 1‑inch storm delivers about 25% of a normal year in hours. reasoned from [1]
  • Arizona permits rainwater harvesting by property owners, but diverting flow in a defined watercourse can raise surface‑water‑right questions; confirm with ADWR before building in washes. open question

Path · Step 4: Bank it

Managed aquifer recharge: check dams, gabions, infiltration basins

Check dams

Low rock or concrete barriers in ephemeral channels slow flow and trap sediment. A USGS study in Arizona's Chiricahua Mountains found check dams reduced peak runoff but did not reduce overall flow volume [27].

Gabions

Wire baskets of rock. A University of Arizona study of an ephemeral stream found gabions raised infiltrated volume by ~10% on average for a ~5‑hour flow, with modeled outcomes ranging from no effect to ~255% [26].

Infiltration basins

Spreading basins on permeable alluvial‑fan soils. In the Hualapai basin, an "infiltration project" was credited with 1,200 AF/yr in the 2021 USGS budget but excluded from the 2024 county update [14].
Reflection: in these valleys, natural aquifer recharge is under 1% of precipitation that falls on them (13,100 AF/yr of ~1.74 million AF/yr across Detrital, Hualapai and Sacramento valleys) [34]. Small structures can nudge that fraction, but the evidence base for large gains is thin and site‑specific.
Evidence · Local precedent

Lake Havasu City's reuse‑water and recharge program

Lake Havasu City was founded by Robert McCulloch in 1963; its first golf course (London Bridge) opened in 1967 [10]. Golf and landscape irrigation later became the main outlet for the city's treated effluent: close to half of effluent (~2,000 AF/yr) went to four golf courses and other irrigators, the rest percolated or injected [9]; about 1,850 AF of effluent was sold for irrigation in 2018 [11].

The recharge program is much newer. In December 2008 the city began injecting ultrafiltered effluent from the North Regional WWTP into four 180‑ft vadose‑zone wells, described as the first treated‑wastewater recharge project along the lower Colorado River [7]. The pilot (Dec 2008–Apr 2009) used 180 AF and built a ~200‑ft‑thick groundwater mound [7]. It followed a sewer expansion replacing almost 28,000 septic tanks [7]. The 2022 Wastewater Master Plan describes a "closed system" where effluent must be reused or recharged, and recommends ~2 MGD of additional vadose wells [8].

Correction — "since 1967". corrected We found no source for a reuse‑water recharge program since 1967. 1967 matches the opening of the city's first golf course [10]. The documented effluent recharge program dates to December 2008 [7]. Suggested wording: "Lake Havasu City has reused treated effluent for irrigation for decades and has recharged it through vadose‑zone wells since 2008."

  • Dec 19, 2022: ADWR establishes the Hualapai Valley Irrigation Non‑Expansion Area; the Director found an AMA "not necessary" (no subsidence or water‑quality evidence at the time) [13]. INAs bar new irrigated acreage and require metering/reporting of non‑exempt wells [12].

Evidence · Yield math

How much water does an acre of rock give?

Derivation. 1 acre = 43,560 ft²; 1 inch = 1/12 ft; 1 ft³ = 7.48052 gal → 43,560 × (1/12) × 7.48052 = 27,154 gal per inch per acre.

At the 1991–2020 normal of 4.16 in [1] and a 40% runoff coefficient: 27,154 × 4.16 × 0.40 = 45,184 gal/yr. The user‑cited ~40,000 gal/yr implies 3.68 in of rain; with the older 1981–2010 normal of 3.84 in [2] it is 41,709 gal.

Check — "1 acre ≈ 40,000 gal/yr at 40%". holds (conservative) Consistent with valley normals (41.7k–45.2k gal). Caveats: the 40% coefficient is an assumption, not measured for Mohave Mountain slopes; the mountain itself likely receives more rain than the city gauge, but no long‑term gauge there was found; and dry years can bring far less.

Scenario: 110°F July

Evaporation losses in extreme heat

July normal highs at Lake Havasu City reach 109°F, with about 178 days a year at or above 90°F [1]. The nearest long pan‑evaporation record (Davis Dam) shows ~101.5 in/yr, peaking at 13.3 in in June [3]; that is roughly 24 times the local rainfall. At the scale of the lake itself, Reclamation estimates Lake Havasu lost an average of 139,626 AF/yr to evaporation in 2017–2021 [4].

Path · Step 5: Rules

Policy context

The 1980 Groundwater Management Act created ADWR and initially designated four Active Management Areas (Phoenix, Pinal, Prescott, Tucson) and two Irrigation Non‑Expansion Areas [30]. Today's AMAs: Phoenix, Pinal, Prescott, Tucson, Santa Cruz, Douglas (2022); INAs: Joseph City, Harquahala, Hualapai Valley [30][31].

New irrigated acreageBarredBarredAllowed

Rural groundwater debate. Bills to create locally led Rural Groundwater Management Areas, backed by Gov. Hobbs, have repeatedly stalled since 2017, including in 2025 [16][33].

Takings‑claim risk. Opponents warn that limiting pumping could be a compensable taking. Arizona's Supreme Court upheld the GMA against such a challenge in Town of Chino Valley v. City of Prescott (1981), holding there is no vested right in groundwater before it is captured [32]. Risk is lower than often claimed, but not zero for novel rules affecting existing uses. legal interpretation, not legal advice
